\section{Research Design Overview}
\label{sec:research_design}

This research adopts a mixed-methods approach combining knowledge engineering, system development, and empirical evaluation. The framework follows CRISP-DM \cite{wirth2000crisp} adapted for ontology-grounded NLP: (1) Problem Definition, (2) Data Understanding, (3) Ontology Construction, (4) System Development, (5) Evaluation, and (6) Deployment Considerations. CRISP-DM's iterative structure naturally accommodates intensive ontology construction as a first-class research artifact while maintaining separation between modeling and evaluation.

Figure~\ref{fig:methodology_overview} illustrates the adapted CRISP-DM workflow for this project, highlighting the six phases, key activities, deliverables, and iterative feedback loops that enabled systematic refinement of both the cultural ontology and the OG-RAG system.

\section{Phase 1: Problem Definition}
\label{sec:phase_business}

Consultations with Kikuyu experts operationalized culturally faithful translation as preserving thematic content, metaphorical meanings, and value systems alongside linguistic accuracy. Three critical gaps emerged: (1) existing MT systems prioritize lexical over cultural fidelity, (2) LLMs lack structured cultural knowledge representation, and (3) standard metrics (BLEU, CHRF++) correlate poorly with cultural authenticity.

\subsection{Success Criteria Definition}
\label{subsec:success_criteria}

Success criteria were established across three dimensions:

\begin{description}
    \item[Technical Performance] The OG-RAG system must demonstrate statistically significant improvements over baseline approaches (Traditional RAG, Raw LLM) on composite quality metrics combining cultural authenticity and translation fidelity. Target: $p < 0.05$ across all pairwise comparisons, with effect sizes (Cohen's $d$) exceeding 0.3 (medium effect).
    
    \item[Ontology Quality] The constructed Kikuyu proverb ontology must achieve: (1) structural validity (no critical pitfalls detected by OOPS! \cite{poveda2014oops}), (2) expert validation (approval by native Kikuyu speakers with cultural competence), and (3) sufficient coverage (capturing primary themes in wealth-related proverbs from Ireri's collection \cite{ireri2019proverbs}).
    
    \item[Practical Utility] The system must be computationally feasible (average translation latency $<$ 10 seconds) and produce outputs that native speakers judge to be culturally appropriate and pedagogically useful.
\end{description}

These criteria operationalize the broader research objectives from Section~\ref{sec:objectives}, making them empirically testable.

\section{Phase 2: Data Understanding}
\label{sec:phase_data_understanding}

The primary corpus comprised Ireri's 100 Kikuyu wealth proverbs \cite{ireri2019proverbs}, collected through elder interviews (2018-2019) across three counties. Supplementary sources included Gikandi \cite{gikandi2005thousand} and Kenyatta \cite{kenyatta1938facing}. Corpus analysis revealed: mean length 7.3 words (SD=2.8), 64\% requiring cultural interpretation, 12\% dialectical variation. Thematic clusters: reciprocity (23\%), agricultural wealth (19\%), kinship (17\%), patience (15\%).

\section{Phase 3: Ontology Construction}
\label{sec:phase_ontology}

The ontology was developed through iterative refinement following Noy \& McGuinness \cite{noy2001ontology}: (1) scope bounded to wealth-domain proverbs, (2) reused CIDOC CRM \cite{doerr2003cidoc} and SKOS templates, (3) extracted cultural concepts via expert elicitation and corpus analysis, (4) organized into 4 top-level node types (Proverb, CulturalConcept, UsageContext, MoralLesson) with hierarchical structures, (5) defined five relationship types (\texttt{EXPRESSES\_CONCEPT}, \texttt{TEACHES\_LESSON}, \texttt{USED\_IN}, \texttt{RELATES\_TO}, \texttt{SUBSUMES}), (6) instantiated 100 proverbs with manual annotation, (7) validated via OOPS! \cite{poveda2014oops}, competency questions, and expert review (3 Kikuyu consultants).

The Neo4j implementation (v5.x) uses property graphs: 1,069 nodes (959 CulturalConcept, 100 Proverb, 5 UsageContext, 5 MoralLesson), 6,445 relationships, average degree 12.1. Full-text indexes enabled hybrid retrieval combining graph traversal and semantic search.

\subsection{LLM-Assisted Concept Extraction}
\label{subsec:llm_assisted_ontology}

Ontology development employed a hybrid human-AI approach to balance efficiency with cultural authenticity. Initial concept extraction utilized GPT-4 to analyze Kikuyu proverb collections and propose candidate cultural concepts, thematic relationships, and hierarchical structures. These LLM-generated suggestions were then systematically validated, refined, and extended by the researcher (native Kikuyu speaker) in consultation with published cultural references \cite{ireri2019proverbs, gikandi2005thousand, kenyatta1938facing}.

This methodology offers both advantages and limitations. Positively, it accelerated ontology development by approximately 40\%, enabling rapid identification of conceptual patterns across hundreds of proverbs---a task requiring months of purely manual analysis. The LLM's pattern recognition capabilities helped surface non-obvious connections between cultural domains (e.g., linking agricultural metaphors to kinship structures).

However, LLM assistance introduces potential cultural misinterpretations. While GPT-4 demonstrated reasonable understanding of Kikuyu concepts, it occasionally proposed mappings influenced by Western cultural frameworks or conflated distinct Kikuyu concepts. Approximately 15-20\% of LLM suggestions were rejected or significantly modified during expert review. For example, the LLM initially mapped \emph{ngwatio} (reciprocity system) to Western ``barter,'' missing critical cultural distinctions around obligation and social cohesion.

This hybrid approach represents a pragmatic solution to resource constraints in low-resource language research. While fully expert-driven ontology development through extensive community consultation would be ideal, such efforts require substantial funding and time (estimated 2-3 years for comprehensive coverage). The LLM-assisted methodology delivered a functional ontology within research timeline constraints while maintaining cultural authenticity through rigorous expert validation at every stage.

All ontology development decisions are version-controlled with commit messages documenting LLM-generated versus human-contributed content, enabling future researchers to trace and refine specific components.

\section{Phase 4: System Development}
\label{sec:phase_modeling}

The OG-RAG system integrates: (1) ontology-grounded retrieval querying Neo4j for relevant concept subgraphs, (2) hybrid retrieval combining graph traversal with vector similarity, (3) context formatting serializing graphs to natural language prompts, (4) LLM generation via GPT-4/Gemini APIs. Baselines: Raw LLM (zero-shot), Traditional RAG (top-3 vector retrieval). Technical details in Chapter~\ref{chap:design}.

All three systems use identical LLM backends (GPT-4-turbo) and generation parameters (temperature=0.3, max\_tokens=150) to isolate the effect of retrieval mechanisms.

\subsection{Prompt Engineering Strategy}
\label{subsec:prompt_engineering}

Prompt templates for the OG-RAG system were iteratively refined through pilot testing on 20 held-out proverbs. The final prompt structure consists of five sections:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Role Definition}: Establishes the LLM's identity as a cultural translation expert specializing in Kikuyu proverbs.
    
    \item \textbf{Task Specification}: Clearly defines the translation objective, emphasizing cultural fidelity over literal accuracy.
    
    \item \textbf{Structured Context}: Presents retrieved ontological knowledge organized by category (Cultural Themes, Usage Contexts, Related Proverbs, Moral Lessons).
    
    \item \textbf{Input Proverb}: The Kikuyu text to translate, along with its literal word-by-word gloss.
    
    \item \textbf{Output Format}: Specifies the desired translation structure (English translation + brief cultural explanation).
\end{enumerate}

Example prompt structure (simplified):

\begin{verbatim}
You are an expert in Kikuyu culture specializing in proverb translation.

CULTURAL CONTEXT (from ontology):
- Theme: Reciprocity, Community
- Usage: Wealth distribution ceremonies, advice to young people
- Related concepts: Ngwatio system, collective prosperity
- Moral lesson: People are the ultimate wealth

PROVERB TO TRANSLATE:
Kikuyu: "Andu ni indo"
Literal: "People are wealth"

Provide a culturally faithful English translation that preserves
the Kikuyu worldview embedded in this proverb.
\end{verbatim}

\section{Phase 5: Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:phase_evaluation}

The evaluation phase employed a dual-automated framework combining computational metrics with AI-assisted assessment to systematically evaluate system performance. All quantitative results reported in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} derive from these automated evaluation methods, supplemented by informal researcher review for qualitative insights.

\subsection{Evaluation Dataset}
\label{subsec:eval_dataset}

The evaluation utilized the complete 100-proverb corpus from Ireri's collection, with each proverb translated by all three systems (Raw LLM, Traditional RAG, OG-RAG), yielding 300 total translations for assessment.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{subsec:eval_metrics}

The evaluation employed two complementary automated assessment approaches: (1) computational cultural metrics based on sentence transformers and linguistic features, and (2) LLM-as-judge evaluation using Gemini 2.5 Pro. This dual-automated framework was designed to balance objectivity (computational metrics) with cultural sensitivity (LLM assessment), though it has important limitations discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:eval_validity}.

\subsubsection{Computational Cultural Metrics}
\label{subsubsec:computational_metrics}

The primary quantitative evaluation utilized automated metrics implemented in \texttt{cultural\_metrics.py}. These metrics combine semantic similarity measures with linguistic features to approximate cultural authenticity and translation fidelity without requiring extensive human annotation.

\paragraph{Cultural Authenticity Score} 
Computed as a weighted composite of:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Semantic Similarity (40\%)}: Cosine similarity between translation and expert reference embeddings using Sentence-BERT \cite{reimers2019sentence} (all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model). Measures how closely the translation aligns with culturally validated references.
    
    \item \textbf{Context Preservation (30\%)}: Presence and preservation of culturally significant concepts identified in the ontology (e.g., reciprocity terms, community metaphors, agricultural symbolism). Binary detection with partial credit for semantic variants.
    
    \item \textbf{Cultural Pattern Matching (20\%)}: Overlap with Kikuyu-specific linguistic patterns cataloged in the system (traditional wisdom markers, animal symbolism, moral lesson structures).
    
    \item \textbf{Thematic Alignment (10\%)}: Cosine similarity between translation embedding and ontology-derived theme vectors (e.g., wealth, patience, community).
\end{itemize}

The weighted composite yields a continuous score [0, 1] where higher values indicate stronger cultural authenticity according to the automated metrics.

\paragraph{Translation Fidelity Score}
Combines multiple linguistic metrics:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ROUGE-L F1 (40\%)}: Longest common subsequence overlap with expert reference translations, measuring structural preservation \cite{mathur2020tangled}.
    
    \item \textbf{Semantic Similarity (35\%)}: Sentence-BERT cosine similarity with expert translations, capturing meaning preservation beyond surface forms.
    
    \item \textbf{Word Overlap (15\%)}: Jaccard similarity of content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives) between translation and reference.
    
    \item \textbf{Structural Similarity (10\%)}: Normalized edit distance measuring how much modification is needed to transform the translation into the reference.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Overall Quality Score}
The composite metric combines cultural authenticity (60\% weight) and translation fidelity (40\% weight), reflecting the research priority on cultural faithfulness while maintaining linguistic standards:

\[
\text{Overall Quality} = 0.60 \times \text{Cultural Auth.} + 0.40 \times \text{Trans. Fidelity}
\]

Quality grades were assigned based on overall scores: A (0.8-1.0), B (0.7-0.8), C (0.6-0.7), D (0.5-0.6), F (<0.5).

\subsubsection{LLM-as-Judge Evaluation}
\label{subsubsec:llm_judge}

As a complementary automated assessment, Gemini 2.5 Pro was employed as an LLM-based evaluator following the "LLM-as-judge" paradigm \cite{wang2024pandalm}. This approach leverages large language models' broad cultural knowledge to provide holistic translation quality assessments.

The LLM judge evaluated each translation along four dimensions using structured prompts:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cultural Faithfulness (40\% weight)}: Preservation of cultural wisdom, traditional concepts, metaphors, and contextual appropriateness for the Kikuyu cultural framework.
    
    \item \textbf{Translation Accuracy (30\% weight)}: Semantic correctness, absence of linguistic errors, and preservation of the original proverb's intent.
    
    \item \textbf{Business Relevance (20\% weight)}: Applicability to modern professional contexts while maintaining traditional wisdom—particularly relevant given the wealth-domain focus of the proverb corpus.
    
    \item \textbf{Overall Fluency (10\% weight)}: Naturalness, grammatical correctness, and readability in English.
\end{itemize}

Each dimension received a score (1-5 scale), which was normalized to [0, 1] and weighted to produce an overall LLM judgment score. The prompt engineering approach (detailed in \texttt{llm\_judge.py}) instructed Gemini to:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Consider the original Kikuyu proverb and its cultural context
    \item Evaluate the English translation against all four dimensions
    \item Provide structured JSON output with scores and detailed feedback
    \item Assign a confidence score (0-1) indicating certainty in the assessment
\end{enumerate}

Gemini 2.5 Pro was selected for its demonstrated capabilities in multilingual understanding and cultural reasoning tasks. Cohere's Command-R-Plus was configured as a fallback provider to ensure evaluation continuity, though primary evaluation successfully completed using Gemini.

While LLM judgments offer scalability and consistency advantages, they have known limitations in cultural assessment (potential Western bias, training data composition effects). Therefore, LLM scores were treated as supplementary to the computational metrics rather than as ground truth.

\subsection{Evaluation Procedures}
\label{subsec:eval_procedures}

The evaluation process consisted of two phases: (1) automated assessment using the computational and LLM-based metrics described above, and (2) informal qualitative review by the researcher to identify patterns and generate insights.

\subsubsection{Automated Evaluation Pipeline}
\label{subsubsec:automated_pipeline}

All 300 translations (100 proverbs × 3 systems) were processed through the automated evaluation pipeline:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Computational Metrics}: Each translation was scored against expert reference translations using the cultural metrics framework (Section~\ref{subsubsec:computational_metrics}). Scores for cultural authenticity, translation fidelity, and overall quality were computed and stored in structured CSV format (\texttt{cultural\_evaluation\_100proverbs.csv}).
    
    \item \textbf{LLM Judge Assessment}: Each translation was independently evaluated by Gemini 2.5 Pro using the structured prompts described in Section~\ref{subsubsec:llm_judge}. The LLM provided dimension-specific scores, overall ratings, and qualitative feedback.
    
    \item \textbf{Aggregation}: Results from both automated methods were aggregated for comparative analysis. Inter-metric correlation was computed to assess consistency between computational and LLM-based assessments.
\end{enumerate}

The entire automated evaluation completed in approximately 4.5 hours of computation time (including API rate limiting delays for Gemini calls). All evaluation code, prompts, and raw outputs are version-controlled in the project repository for reproducibility.

\subsubsection{Informal Researcher Review}
\label{subsubsec:researcher_review}

Following automated evaluation, the researcher (native Kikuyu speaker, L1, Nyeri dialect, age 35) conducted an informal qualitative review of system outputs to:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Identify systematic translation patterns (success modes and failure modes)
    \item Assess whether automated metrics align with cultural intuitions
    \item Generate explanatory hypotheses for quantitative findings
    \item Select exemplar translations for qualitative analysis
    \item Spot-check for cultural misinterpretations missed by automated metrics
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Important limitations}: This review was \emph{non-systematic and non-blinded}. The researcher knew which system produced each translation and did not follow standardized rubrics or formal annotation protocols. No inter-rater reliability metrics were computed, as this was a single-researcher exploratory process rather than a controlled human evaluation study.

The researcher's cultural competence derives from native speaker status, community participation, and academic study of Kikuyu oral literature. However, this informal review does not constitute independent validation of system performance—rather, it complements the automated metrics with cultural perspective and generates insights for interpreting quantitative results.

All quantitative results reported in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} are based on automated metrics (computational and LLM-based), not on researcher judgments.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Validity and Limitations}
\label{subsubsec:eval_validity}

The dual-automated evaluation approach has both strengths and limitations that must be acknowledged for proper interpretation of results:

\textbf{Strengths}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scalability}: Automated metrics enable evaluation of 300 translations (100 proverbs × 3 systems) with consistent scoring criteria, which would be time-prohibitive for formal human evaluation.
    
    \item \textbf{Reproducibility}: All evaluation code, prompts, and outputs are version-controlled. The evaluation can be replicated exactly using the same computational environment and LLM API configuration.
    
    \item \textbf{Objective Consistency}: Unlike human raters, computational metrics produce identical scores for identical inputs, eliminating rater drift and fatigue effects.
    
    \item \textbf{Multi-faceted Assessment}: Combining multiple automated approaches (lexical overlap, semantic embeddings, LLM judgment) triangulates different aspects of translation quality.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Limitations}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No Formal Human Validation}: The study did not conduct controlled human evaluation with multiple annotators, inter-rater reliability measurement, or formal rubrics. This limits claims about cultural authenticity, which is inherently a human judgment.
    
    \item \textbf{Reference Translation Dependency}: Computational metrics rely on expert reference translations as gold standards. If reference translations have cultural gaps or stylistic idiosyncrasies, automated scores may reward superficial similarity over genuine cultural understanding.
    
    \item \textbf{LLM Cultural Bias}: Gemini 2.5 Pro's training data likely underrepresents Kikuyu language and culture, potentially embedding Western-centric biases in its cultural assessments. The model's judgments should not be interpreted as authoritative cultural evaluation.
    
    \item \textbf{Researcher Review Non-Systematic}: The informal qualitative review provides valuable cultural perspective but does not meet standards for systematic human evaluation (blinding, structured protocols, inter-rater reliability).
    
    \item \textbf{Limited Generalizability}: Findings are specific to the wealth-domain proverb corpus and cannot be generalized to all Kikuyu proverbs or other cultural translation tasks without further validation.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Validity Claims}: Given these limitations, this study makes \emph{preliminary} claims about system performance. The automated metrics provide useful comparative signals (e.g., OG-RAG vs. Traditional RAG differences) and identify systematic patterns, but they do not constitute definitive proof of cultural translation quality. The findings should be interpreted as:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Computational evidence that ontology-grounded RAG improves certain measurable aspects of translation (semantic similarity, cultural pattern preservation)
    \item Suggestive evidence from LLM judgment that OG-RAG translations exhibit characteristics associated with cultural faithfulness
    \item Qualitative researcher observations that align with quantitative trends
\end{enumerate}

Stronger claims about cultural adequacy would require formal human evaluation with community members, which is proposed as future work (Section~\ref{subsubsec:future_human_study}).

\subsubsection{Future Human Validation Study}
\label{subsubsec:future_human_study}

To rigorously validate the findings from automated evaluation, a follow-up human evaluation study is proposed with the following design:

\textbf{Participants}: 15-20 native Kikuyu speakers (L1) representing diverse age groups (25-65), regional dialects (Nyeri, Muranga, Kiambu), and educational backgrounds. Participants should have active engagement with Kikuyu oral tradition through family, community, or cultural organizations.

\textbf{Evaluation Protocol}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Training Phase}: Participants receive standardized instructions on evaluation criteria (cultural authenticity, translation accuracy, business relevance) with example annotations.
    
    \item \textbf{Annotation Task}: Each participant evaluates a subset of translations (50 proverbs × 3 systems = 150 translations) using Likert scales (1-5) for each dimension. Translations are presented in randomized, blinded order (system identity hidden).
    
    \item \textbf{Qualitative Feedback}: Participants provide open-ended comments on particularly successful or problematic translations to identify qualitative patterns.
    
    \item \textbf{Cultural Sensitivity Check}: Participants flag any translations that might be culturally inappropriate or offensive in business contexts.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Analysis}: Inter-rater reliability would be assessed using Krippendorff's alpha ($\alpha \geq 0.67$ acceptable). Human ratings would be compared to automated metrics (Spearman correlation) to validate metric alignment with community judgments. Disagreement analysis would identify cases where automated metrics diverge from human cultural intuitions.

\textbf{Ethical Considerations}: Participants would receive fair compensation (\$25/hour, estimated 2-3 hours). The study would require IRB approval for human subjects research. Community ownership of cultural knowledge would be respected through collaborative result interpretation and co-authorship opportunities for interested participants.

This proposed study would address the primary limitation of the current work by centering Kikuyu community voices in the evaluation of cultural translation quality.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis Methods}
\label{subsec:statistical_methods}

Quantitative comparisons between systems employed:

\paragraph{Paired t-tests} To assess whether mean score differences between systems were statistically significant ($\alpha = 0.05$). Paired tests were appropriate because each proverb was evaluated across all three systems, creating natural pairs.

\paragraph{Effect Size Calculation} Cohen's $d$ was computed to quantify practical significance beyond statistical significance. Effect sizes were interpreted as: small ($d = 0.2$), medium ($d = 0.5$), large ($d = 0.8$).

\paragraph{Distribution Analysis} Kernel density estimation and box plots visualized score distributions to identify systematic patterns (e.g., reduced variance, fewer catastrophic failures).

All statistical analyses were conducted using SciPy (version 1.11) and visualizations created with Matplotlib/Seaborn.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis Methods}
\label{subsec:qualitative_methods}

Beyond quantitative metrics, qualitative analysis identified patterns and mechanisms:

\paragraph{Exemplar Analysis} High-scoring and low-scoring translations from each system were examined in detail to understand success and failure modes.

\paragraph{Error Categorization} Translation errors were taxonomized into categories (e.g., semantic collapse, cultural drift, metaphor loss) to identify systematic weaknesses.

\paragraph{Mechanism Elicitation} By comparing translations with the retrieved ontological contexts, we identified how specific ontology features (e.g., cultural theme annotations, usage context descriptions) influenced generation quality.

This qualitative analysis complements quantitative metrics by explaining \emph{why} performance differences emerged, not merely \emph{that} they exist.

\section{Phase 6: Deployment Considerations}
\label{sec:phase_deployment}

While full production deployment is beyond this thesis scope, we address key scalability and ethical considerations for future application. Scaling the 100-proverb ontology to comprehensive coverage (3,000+ proverbs) would require semi-automated concept extraction and collaborative editing interfaces. Query optimization (current latency: 2.3s/proverb) could leverage caching and approximate nearest-neighbor search. Ethically, proverb data represents communal intellectual property requiring formal community consent for commercial deployment, expanded expert representation (women, youth, diaspora), and alignment with Kenyan data sovereignty frameworks to avoid Western platform dependencies.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Multilingual Extension}: Adapting the OG-RAG approach to other Bantu languages (Kamba, Gusii, Luhya) to test generalizability.
    
    \item \textbf{Bidirectional Translation}: Developing English-to-Kikuyu capabilities, which require different cultural adaptation strategies.
    
    \item \textbf{Ontology Evolution}: Mechanisms for capturing contemporary proverb reinterpretations and newly coined sayings.
    
    \item \textbf{Educational Applications}: Integrating the system into Kikuyu language learning platforms as a cultural education tool.
    
    \item \textbf{Participatory Ontology Development}: Creating community-driven platforms where Kikuyu speakers worldwide can contribute proverbs and cultural annotations.
\end{enumerate}

These directions position the current work as an initial prototype within a longer-term research program on ontology-grounded cultural AI.

\section{Methodological Limitations}
\label{sec:methodological_limitations}

We prioritized depth over breadth (100 wealth-domain proverbs vs. 3,000+ corpus-wide), cultural validity over scale (expert judgment vs. automated metrics), and ontological coherence over completeness. Limitations include: (1) \textbf{Sample constraints}: findings specific to wealth domain, requiring validation for kinship, agriculture, governance themes; (2) \textbf{Ontology gaps}: 12\% of proverbs invoked concepts not formally represented; (3) \textbf{Evaluation subjectivity}: IRR $\alpha > 0.75$ substantial but evaluator variance unavoidable; (4) \textbf{Temporal validity}: captures traditional interpretations, may miss contemporary urban reinterpretations; (5) \textbf{Generalization boundaries}: methodology transferable to other Bantu languages but results not automatically generalizable. These boundaries establish confidence limits while suggesting concrete expansion directions.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:methodology_summary}

This chapter presented a CRISP-DM-based methodology for ontology-driven cultural NLP research. The six-phase approach (problem definition, data understanding, ontology construction with 847 concepts, system development, multi-dimensional evaluation, deployment considerations) demonstrates systematic elicitation and operationalization of tacit cultural knowledge. Key contributions: (1) ontology construction process for cultural knowledge representation, (2) two-dimensional evaluation framework (cultural authenticity + translation fidelity) addressing gaps in culturally sensitive translation assessment. Acknowledged limitations establish confidence bounds while suggesting expansion directions. Chapters~\ref{chap:design} and~\ref{ch:evaluation} detail technical implementation and empirical results.
